About

Why Kettle exists

Kettle builds small tools for repeatable jobs, each published with what was tested and where it broke. Inside the codebase each tool is called a Cup.

The question Kettle starts with

For any repeatable job, Kettle asks what's the smallest thing that does it well enough: plain code, a small specialist model, a local language model, or a remote model. All four are fair answers. "Use what your computer already has" and "just use a remote model" are results worth publishing too.

Running locally doesn't make a tool good by itself. Operating systems already ship their own models for everyday writing, so a local tool still has to earn its keep on real files. When the source is already public — like funders' websites in the funding watch — running in the cloud makes more sense.

Why local

I put Qwen 2.5 on a Raspberry Pi as a proof of concept. It worked, slowly, and gave a decent answer about fixing a tap. That got me thinking: local models have a place doing everyday jobs that don't need frontier AI. I care about privacy, energy, water and the communities living next to data centres, and the scale of spending going into AI makes me wonder whether a smaller approach could cost less. Those are the reasons I started building Kettle.

You don't need all of those reasons to want a local tool; one is enough. How it works covers how the tools are built and how privacy is checked, and the research pages record what has actually been tested.

No chatbots inside

None of these tools are chatbots. You don't chat with them, they don't write answers in their own words, and they don't send, book, file or call anything for you. Every tool stops at a draft for you to check, because the main risk with models that generate text is a smooth, confident mistake — like an invented date or amount in your own paperwork — that's easy to miss. Most of the tools need neither generative AI nor any model at all.

What Kettle doesn't claim

  • Energy or carbon reductions. Energy hasn't been measured, and tokens count model demand, not electricity.
  • Better models. Operating systems and large labs will always lead on model quality.
  • Accuracy percentages or reliability figures. A handful of development examples can't give you those.
  • A replacement for a frontier model beyond the specific jobs tested.

The research pages cover what's been tried so far, including the study where an agent guide made a frontier model do more work, not less.
