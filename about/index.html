About

Local or remote, and what each task needs.

Kettle Cups builds small local tools for repeatable tasks, and publishes a check of what each one replaces and where it fails.

The question I ask

The question is local or remote, not AI or no AI. For a repeatable task, what is the smallest thing that does it well enough: ordinary code, a small specialist model, a local language model or a remote model? All four are legitimate answers. “Use what your computer already does” and “use the remote model” are also results worth publishing.

Being local does not, by itself, make a tool worth having. Computers now come with models of their own for everyday writing tasks. Each tool comes with a record of what was tested and what failed.

No chatbots inside

Right now none of the tools uses a chatbot or a large language model. Those models write new text, and that is where confident mistakes come from: a made-up name, date or amount in your own paperwork is easy to miss. The nearest thing is the speech model in Meeting Notes. It writes out what it hears and can occasionally write words nobody said, which is why you check its transcript against the recording.

A future tool might use a generative model where a task really needs one. Mostly, this site is here to show that many everyday jobs need neither generative AI nor any model at all. Text and Regulars are ordinary code, and Tables reads PDF statements without a model.

Why local

Using Qwen 2.5 on a Raspberry Pi got me thinking. It was slow, but it answered a question about fixing a tap. I think local models have a place doing everyday jobs that don’t need frontier AI. I care about privacy, energy, water and the communities hosting data centres. The huge investment planned also makes me question whether a more balanced approach could cost less. These are reasons for building the tools.

There is more than one reason to run a task on your own computer, and a tool can be worth having for one of them alone. For each reason, this is what has been checked so far.

Privacy
Checked. Your file stays on your computer. Tables read real examples with outside network connections denied by the operating system. What its underlying libraries do when unrestricted has not been checked. See the claims and checks.
Remote requests
Counted. No remote inference was required for the tested executions.
Model work
Measured once: a token budget for one small table.
Quality and effort
Tested on named examples and in my own use only, including the effort of finding and correcting mistakes.
Energy, water and the places that host data centres
Not measured here. They are part of why I care where this work runs. I claim no result.

How the tools work

  • You finish the task and check it. You see the source beside the draft, correct it and save something exact.
  • Models are named, with exact versions and file identities.
  • Setup is the only network step. Normal use downloads nothing.
  • A tool with fixed model files gives the same answer next year, and has no charge for each use.

What I do not claim

  • Energy or carbon reduction. I have not measured energy. Tokens describe demand on a model, not energy.
  • Better models. Operating systems and the large providers will lead on model quality.
  • Reliability figures. Development examples cannot give them.
  • A stand-in for a frontier model, beyond the named cases a tool was tested on.

The research pages show the results so far, including a test where my agent guide used more model work, not less.

Who builds it

Kettle Cups grew out of Kettle, an earlier experiment in reading paperwork with local models. It keeps the focus on small tasks with results you can inspect and correct. Tables is the first tool.
