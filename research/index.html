Research

What Kettle tried, and what happened

Before building a tool, Kettle tests whether the approach actually works on real examples — including the ones where the idea didn't hold up.

You don't need any of this to use the tools.

So far

Testing a small legal model on complaint letters

12 October 2026

Across four letters, a small legal model invented nothing while a general model made up two dates. I preferred the legal model's drafts, and still decided not to build the tool because drafting from notes is already what chatbots are good at. The part I kept was the fact-check script.

Limits: four letters to one housing association, notes generated by an AI assistant, and I didn't run a chatbot alongside them.

Hand-written rules against small models for reading picture requests

6 October 2026

On 60 short typed requests about a picture, hand-written rules got 59 right while three small embedding models managed 47 or 48. The next day a form-filling language model hit 56. I shipped rules plus that model, then took both out two days later for a simpler form.

Limits: AI models wrote all the test requests, which flatters the rules; nothing typed by a person was tested.

Giving Claude Code a guide to Tables

19 September 2026

Given a guide and my local Tables reader, Claude Code used more tokens and took longer than it did just looking at the picture — and got one fewer of seven small tables right. The plain run still gave a concrete figure for what one small table costs an agent.

Limits: seven small invented tables, one agent, one pass.

What's been checked on Tables

10 to 14 September 2026

4 of 5 named synthetic tables exact; unruled adds an empty row. Two cropped phone photos came back exact. In my own trial, mistakes still slipped through in two prepared correction tasks.

Limits: development examples and my own use only. Nobody else has tried it, and Kettle doesn't report an accuracy percentage.

Kettle hasn't measured energy anywhere here, and claims no energy reductions. Tokens measure demand on a model, not electricity.

How Kettle keeps the evidence straight

How checks and claims are kept separate
  • Software checks show the code does what it says — like quoting CSV cells properly. They tell you nothing about whether OCR read your table right.
  • Model runs only speak for the exact model files and inputs tested.
  • Real use is what happens when a person actually runs the tool, including the mistakes they miss.

Kettle also keeps three questions separate, because answering one doesn't answer the others: is the tool useful? Does the whole workflow use less compute at the same quality? Does publishing the findings help anyone else? Right now there is some evidence for the first (from my own use), a token budget for one small task (not a measured reduction), and nothing yet for the third.
